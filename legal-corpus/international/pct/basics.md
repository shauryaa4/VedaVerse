The PCT is not itself a grant of patent rights -- there is no such thing as a "PCT patent." It is a filing-route treaty: a single international application, filed with one receiving office (for an Indian applicant, ordinarily the Indian Patent Office) in one language, has the effect of a national filing in every one of the PCT's contracting states (over 150) as of the application's international filing date.

Basic route for an Indian Ayurvedic-product applicant considering international protection:
1. File an international application under the PCT (optionally after first filing in India and claiming priority within 12 months, per the Paris Convention priority mechanism).
2. An International Search Report and a non-binding written opinion on patentability are produced (roughly by month 16 from priority).
3. Optionally request international preliminary examination (Chapter II).
4. Enter the "national phase" in each country where protection is actually wanted, typically by 30-31 months from the priority date -- this is where each country's own patentability law (e.g. a US, EU, or Indian examiner applying their own substantive standard) actually decides whether to grant.

Practical read: the PCT buys time (up to ~30 months instead of 12) and defers cost, but does not change the substantive patentability bar in any country -- an Ayurvedic formulation barred by India's Patents Act §3(p) domestically will not become patentable in India merely by routing through the PCT, and a foreign patent office's own equivalent exclusions (if any) still apply at national phase entry in that country. This is the right citation when a user asks "should I file a PCT application" -- it answers "how," not "whether it's patentable."
